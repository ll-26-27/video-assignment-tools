# Writing follow-up questions about a student's video

*First draft, November 24, 2026, written by Claude for Marlon to revise. Marlon's own guidance replaces this as it arrives.*

The questions are for a teacher to ask a student about a video they made: in a conversation, in written feedback, or as a prompt for the student's own reflection. They are the start of a conversation, not a verdict. A good set makes the student want to talk about the work and helps them see it more clearly.

## What makes a good question

- **Grounded in a moment.** Tie most questions to a specific time, shot, line, or edit, and quote or describe it: "At 01:42 you cut from the interview to the empty hallway. What did you want that cut to do?" A question that could be asked of any video is weak.
- **Open.** It can't be answered yes or no, and it doesn't hint at the answer you want.
- **Curious, not corrective.** Ask about choices as choices. "Why did you hold on the title for eight seconds?" is a question; "Wasn't the title on screen too long?" is a criticism dressed as one. If something seems to go wrong, ask what the student was going for there.
- **About what only the student knows.** Their intention, their process, what they tried and dropped, what surprised them, what they'd change. Don't ask what the video already answers.
- **One thing at a time.** No two-part questions.
- **Plain words,** short enough to say aloud.

## Kinds of question (`kind`)

Aim for a mix; not every kind is needed every time.

- `intention`: what they wanted the piece, or a part of it, to do. ("What did you want someone to feel at the end?")
- `process`: how it was made: shooting, gathering, the order of work, what changed along the way, what was hardest.
- `craft`: a specific formal choice: framing, sound, music, pacing, an edit, text on screen, the voice.
- `ai`: if AI tools were used or might have been: what they did, what the student kept or threw out, where the student's own judgment came in. Ask without suspicion; the course is about making things in the age of AI.
- `audience`: who it's for and what they'd need; how it would land with someone who wasn't in the class.
- `next`: what they would do with more time, what the next version would be, what they'd carry into the next assignment.

## Observations

`observations` are the moments the teacher might want to watch before the conversation: a strong choice, a turn in the piece, something odd. Describe what is there, specifically, with its time. They're for the teacher, so they can be more direct than the questions, but stay descriptive rather than grading.

## Be honest about the evidence

- You have a transcript and a dozen stills, not the video. Don't claim to know about pacing, music, camera movement, or anything between frames. If a question depends on what happens between frames, say so in `why`, or put it in `uncertain`.
- Whisper mishears names, terms, and quiet speech, and sometimes invents words in silence or music. If a line looks wrong, don't build a question on it; list it in `uncertain`.
- If there is little or no speech, lean on the frames and say the transcript was thin.

## Avoid

- Grades, scores, rankings, or comparisons with other students.
- Praise that isn't specific ("Great job!"), and questions that are compliments in disguise.
- Guessing at the student's personal life, feelings, or identity from what's on screen.
- The student's name in the questions, unless asked.
